This section details the experiments used to evaluate whether foundation time series models can accurately forecast ED occupancy. A direct comparison with the baseline models from Tuominen et al. ~\cite{tuominen_forecasting_2024} requires the use of identical data and evaluation metrics.

First, the ED occupancy dataset and external covariates are described. Second, the experimental setup for 24-hour zero-shot forecasting task and different context lengths and external covariate setups are described. Used hardware and measurements for computational costs are also introduced. Third, the evaluation metrics are defined. Finally, all five foundation models and four benchmark models are described. ~\cite{tuominen_forecasting_2024}

\section{Data}

Tuominen et al. ~\cite{tuominen_forecasting_2024} used ED occupancy as a forecasting metric. To directly compare results with Tuominen et al., this thesis uses ED occupancy as its crowding metric. All patients from all ED treatment spaces are summed up to this metric. All parts of the input-throughput-output model are affected in occupancy, which makes it useful. ~\cite{tuominen_forecasting_2024}

The ED occupancy data used in this thesis comes from Tampere University Hospital's (TAYS) electronic health record. TAYS is an academic, Level 1 trauma center in Tampere, Finland. When the study by Tuominen et al.~\cite{tuominen_forecasting_2024} was conducted, TAYS served a primary population of 535,000 within the Pirkanmaa Hospital District and provided tertiary care for a total catchment population of 900,700. The hospital's combined emergency department, Acuta, managed an annual census of approximately 100,000 patients at that time. The ED had a total capacity of 111–118 patients, which included 70 beds (plus 7 reserve) and 41 seats for ambulatory patients. ~\cite{tuominen_forecasting_2024}

The ED occupancy data spans from January 1, 2017, to June 19, 2019. It consists of hourly registered ED visits. As by Tuominen et al. ~\cite{tuominen_forecasting_2024}, a whole year of occupancy data from June 20, 2018, to June 19, 2019, was used as a testing dataset in this thesis. Tuominen et al. also used a total of 167 external covariates. All of the covariate categories are described in table~\ref{tab:covariates}. Covariates can be divided into categories of past and future covariates. Future covariates include data points for the time steps for which the forecasts are made. For example, weather forecasts and public events can be used as future covariates. ~\cite{tuominen_forecasting_2024}

\section{Experimental setup}

The model's task is to predict hourly occupancy levels for the next 24 hours. For a one year testing dataset, this means 365 predictions. All of the predictions are grouped together and compared against the ground truth values from the testing dataset. These results are then directly compared with results from Tuominen et al. ~\cite{tuominen_forecasting_2024}.

To evaluate univariate model performances across different historical window sizes, a set ten different context lengths were tested. These were grouped into temporal and fixed-token windows. The temporal contexts included calendar based intervals of 1 day, 3 days, 1 week, 2 weeks, 4 weeks, and 1 year. The maximum available window size from January 1, 2017 to June 19, 2018 spanned a total of 12840 hours or 535 days, was also included. To fill the gaps in between these window sizes, fixed-length windows of 512, 2048 and 4096 hours were also evaluated.

To test which kind of external covariates foundation models can leverage, the covariates were divided into subsets according to their categories. To enable a direct comparison with Tuominen et al. ~\cite{tuominen_forecasting_2024}, one forecasting was also performed with all covariates grouped together. Adding all the covariates dramatically increases the input dimensionality. This, in turn, rapidly scales the computational load especially with longer context lengths. This is why only context lengths 256, 512 and 1024 hours were tested.

Univariate forecasts were performed on a local workstation equipped with an Intel Core i5-8600K (6-core @ 4.3 GHz) CPU and 16 GB of DDR4 RAM. Multivariate forecasts were computed on the Puhti supercomputer provided by CSC - IT Center for Science, Finland. This experiment was run on one NVIDIA Volta V100 GPU with 32 GB of VRAM.

For evaluation computational requirements of each foundation model, time needed to make forecasts and peak RAM during inference were measured. Time it takes to generate forecasts is called \emph{inference time}. Inference time was measured for the whole test set spanning one year. Peak RAM usage is measured over the whole inference run, including loading the model to the computer memory.

\section{Evaluation}

In this thesis same metrics are used as in Tuominen et al. ~\cite{tuominen_forecasting_2024}.
They used mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean squared error (RMSE) for point forecasts.

MAE is defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MAE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{t=1}^{n} | y_t - \hat{y}_t | \, ,
\end{equation}
where $y_t$ is the ground truth value and $\hat{y}_t$ is the prediction made by the model. RMSE is defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{n} \sum_{t=1}^{n} (y_t - \hat{y}_t)^2 } \, ,
\end{equation}
using the same notation.

MAE measures forecast accuracy in the same units as the data and treats all errors equally ~\cite{hyndman2018forecasting}.
This makes it easy to interpret. A MAE of 5 means forecasts are off by 5 patients on average.

RMSE squares the errors before averaging, which gives more weight to larger errors ~\cite{hyndman2018forecasting}.
This makes RMSE more sensitive to outliers and particularly useful when large errors are more harmful than small ones.
In ED occupancy forecasting, this property is valuable because large prediction errors could lead to understaffing or overstaffing. This could allow crowding or wasted resources.

\section{Models}
\label{sec:models}

This section describes the baseline models from Tuominen et al. ~\cite{tuominen_forecasting_2024} and the FMs selected for this study. The FMs are compared using their architecture and key forecasting parameters.
The \emph{context length} is the number of past time steps the model uses to make a prediction. The \emph{forecasting horizon} is the number of future time steps it predicts.
All selected models have promising zero-shot capabilities, meaning they can be used to make forecasts without any additional training.

The foundation models were selected based on three criteria: open-source availability, ease of use, and performance on the GIFT-Eval benchmark ~\cite{aksu2024giftevalbenchmarkgeneraltime}. 
All chosen models have promising zero-shot capabilities and are available for download from GitHub. The models were downloaded from GitHub in September 2025. The exact model version names and links to their GitHub repositories are listed in Table~\ref{tab:models}. Median predictions were chosen over mean predictions for forecasts.

\subsection{TimesFM}

Developed by Google Research, \emph{TimesFM} is a family of decoder-only transformer models designed for efficient forecasting ~\cite{das_decoder-only_2024}.
The model uses a technique called patching. Patching divides the input time series into fixed-length segments.
The model processes these segments instead of individual time points.
TimesFM uses an input patch length of 32 time steps and an output patch length of 128 time steps.
This reduces the number of steps required for long-horizon forecasting.
Training minimizes the Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss on point forecasts.

The latest version, TimesFM 2.5 that was used in this thesis was released in September 2025. It has 200 million parameters and maximum context length of 16,384.
The model was pretrained on approximately 100 billion time points from diverse sources. These included Google Trends, Wikimedia pageview statistics, and public datasets such as M4 and Electricity.
This data was augmented with synthetic data from ARMA processes.
As of September 2025, TimesFM 2.5 holds third place on the GIFT-Eval leaderboard ~\cite{GIFT-Eval_Leaderboard}.

\subsection{Chronos} 

\emph{Chronos} is a family of foundational time series models developed by Amazon Science ~\cite{ansari_chronos_2024}.
It adopts a language modeling framework and treats time series forecasting as a next-token prediction task.
Based on the T5 transformer architecture ~\cite{Raffel2020T5}, Chronos processes continuous time series data by scaling and quantizing it into a fixed vocabulary of discrete bins.
The model is trained using cross-entropy loss to predict the next token. This frames forecasting as a classification problem at each time step.
To generate forecasts, the model predicts one token at a time. Each predicted token is used as input for predicting the next one.
These discrete tokens are then dequantized and inverse-scaled to produce continuous forecast values.
The model samples multiple forecast paths to generate a prediction distribution. From this distribution it calculates the mean, median, and quantile forecasts.

In this thesis newer version \emph{chronos-bolt-base} was used. It is a 205 million parameter variant of the Chronos family ~\cite{amazon_chronos_bolt_base}.
This model improves upon original architecture by extending the maximum context length from 512 to 2048, while maintaining maximum prediction horizon of 64 ~\cite{ansari2025chronos2}.

\emph{Chronos-bolt-base} processes inputs and generates outputs differently than older versions of Chronos.
It groups input time series into patches processing them as continuous vectors rather than discrete tokens.
This allows model to handle longer context lengths more efficiently.
Outputs are not generated autoregressively as in previous versions, but the whole forecast distribution is generated by one forward pass. ~\cite{ansari_chronos_2024}

The training data was augmented using the TSMIXup and KernelSynth algorithms.
TSMixup creates new time series by combining existing ones. KernelSynth generates synthetic data using Gaussian processes.
As of September 2025, \emph{chronos-bolt-base}, holds fourth place in GIFT-Eval benchmark for models with training data overlap to the test set ~\cite{GIFT-Eval_Leaderboard}.

\subsection{Sundial}

\emph{Sundial} was developed by Tsinghua University in February 2025 ~\cite{liu_sundial_2025}.
Defining characteristic of Sundial is that it outputs probabilistic predictions directly. This is done by producing multiple forecast samples, where mean median and quantile forecasts are calculated.
The prediction mechanism is autoregressive at the patch level.

Sundial uses a decoder-only transformer architecture and works directly on continuous values rather than using quantization.
The model processes time series data in patches of size 16. It is trained using a novel TimeFlow Loss function.

The version of Sundial used has a context length of 2880 but can handle shorter, arbitrary context lengths.
The model was trained on TimeBench. This is a large-scale dataset created by the authors, which contains over a trillion data points from real-world sources like weather and finance.
As of November 2025, Sundial holds the 10th place on the GIFT-Eval leaderboard ~\cite{GIFT-Eval_Leaderboard}.

\subsection{Moirai}

Developed by Salesforce AI Research, \emph{Moirai} features a masked encoder transformer architecture designed specifically for heterogeneous, multivariate time series ~\cite{woo_unified_2024}.
Its key feature is using varying patch sizes based on data frequency. Higher-frequency data uses larger patches.
Each patch is processed by a linear layer and assigned a time ID and a variable ID.
These IDs inject positional information into the transformer through rotary embeddings and learnable scalars.
The transformer's output patches are projected to produce parameters for a mixture of probability distributions.
These parameters include the weights for combining the distributions into a final weighted sum.
This sum forms the final probabilistic forecast.

This study uses the \emph{Moirai 2.0 small} variant, released in August 2025 ~\cite{Salesforce_Moirai20}. 
Unlike its predecessor, this model utilizes a decoder-only transformer architecture with a single patch size, processing the input time series as a sequence of uniform patches. 
This design choice simplifies the input projection while retaining the ability to model complex temporal dependencies.

A key architectural distinction of Moirai 2.0 is its forecast generation mechanism.
Instead of projecting outputs into a mixture of probability distributions, the model uses recursive multi-quantile decoding.
This approach directly predicts the quantiles of the forecast distribution at each step, enhancing both probabilistic accuracy and inference speed.

The model contains approximately 12.4 million parameters, which is 96 \% smaller than the original Moirai Large.
It was trained on a robust corpus comprising the GIFT-Eval dataset, internal Salesforce operational data, and synthetic time series generated via the KernelSynth and TSMixup algorithms.
As of September 2025, Moirai 2.0 small ranks sixth on the GIFT-Eval leaderboard ~\cite{GIFT-Eval_Leaderboard}.

\subsection{TiRex}

\emph{TiRex} was developed by NXAI GmbH in collaboration with academic researchers ~\cite{auer_tirex_2025}. It was released on May 2025.
It is the only foundation model in this thesis based on the xLSTM architecture rather than a transformer architecture ~\cite{beck2024xlstm}.

TiRex normalizes inputs using z-score normalization and processes data in patches of size 32.
Input patches are projected into an embedding dimension by a residual block.
This embedding is fed into the xLSTM.
A second residual block projects the xLSTM output back into the patch dimension to produce forecasts.
The outputs are scaled back to the original target space. The model predicts nine quantiles for each time step.

TiRex has approximately 35 million parameters, making it computationally efficient.
Its xLSTM architecture propagates sequentially hidden states between patches. This removes hard constraints on context length present in transformers.
A context length of 2048 was used for training in the original study, but theoretically the model supports indefinitely large context lengths during inference.

The model's training data comprises 47.5 million time series from Chronos's training data, synthetic data similar to KernelSynth, and parts of the GiftEval pre-training dataset.
As of November 2025, TiRex holds second place on the GIFT-Eval leaderboard among models with training data overlap to the test set ~\cite{GIFT-Eval_Leaderboard}.

\subsection{Benchmark models}

The Seasonal Naïve (SN) model provides a baseline forecast by repeating the last observed value from the previous seasonal period. For a time series with a daily pattern, the forecast for a future time is simply the value observed at the same time one seasonal cycle ago (e.g., 24 hours prior). This approach is a standard benchmark for strongly seasonal data. ~\cite{hyndman2018forecasting}

ARIMAX (Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average with eXogenous inputs) is a statistical model that extends the standard ARIMA method. It combines ARIMA, which models the time series's own autocorrelation structure, with a linear regression component. This regression component allows the model to incorporate the predictive information from external, or exogenous, variables. ~\cite{hyndman2018forecasting}

LightGBM (Light Gradient Boosting Machine) is a machine learning algorithm based on gradient boosting decision trees. It is recognized for its high efficiency and scalability, which makes it particularly effective for large-scale datasets. Tuominen et al. ~\cite{tuominen_forecasting_2024} applied LightGBM in their study forecasting ED occupancy, where it achieved the strongest performance among the models evaluated. ~\cite{ke2017lightgbm}

The Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) is a deep neural network architecture designed specifically for multi-horizon time series forecasting ~\cite{lim2021temporal}. It utilizes an attention-based encoder-decoder structure to learn complex temporal relationships at different scales. A key feature of TFT is its integrated interpretability, which allows for identifying significant past time steps and important features.
