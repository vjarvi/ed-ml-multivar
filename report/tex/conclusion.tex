Emergency department (ED) crowding worsens clinical outcomes and strains staff and resources. This thesis examined whether recently released foundational time series models (FMs) can forecast hourly ED occupancy accurately enough in a zero-shot setting to be useful in practice. Using Tampere University Hospital’s ED (Acuta) data and the experimental protocol aligned with prior work, we compared five open-source FMs against established baselines and assessed how context length, external covariates, and computational requirements affect their utility.

First, we evaluated predictive accuracy. All univariate FMs except Chronos moderately outperformed the strongest baseline (LightGBM). TimesFM achieved the best overall accuracy, while TiRex, Sundial, and Moirai were close when given similarly long histories. Chronos underperformed, likely due to its shorter maximum context. Increasing context length consistently improved accuracy across models, with diminishing returns beyond roughly 2,048 hours. These results show that zero-shot FMs can deliver competitive—and in most cases superior—accuracy for ED occupancy forecasting without any site-specific training.

Finally, we measured computational costs. Moirai was the most memory-efficient model. Chronos produced the fastest inferences at short contexts, while TiRex and Moirai were fastest at longer contexts. Inference times for all models were practical for hourly operational use on modern hardware. These findings indicate that zero-shot FMs can reduce the operational burden associated with frequent model retraining, enabling faster experimentation and easier deployment across sites.

This work has limitations. We studied a single hospital over a fixed period and evaluated one FM with multivariate inputs. We focused on zero-shot use; fine-tuning and lightweight adaptation were out of scope. We did not evaluate calibration, robustness to distribution shift, or the downstream operational impact of forecast-driven interventions. Future research should (i) replicate across multiple hospitals and time periods; (ii) test adapter-based fine-tuning and prompt conditioning; (iii) investigate covariate selection, forecasting, and representation strategies; (iv) evaluate probabilistic calibration and reliability under shift; and (v) run prospective, real-time studies measuring staffing, patient flow, and safety outcomes.

In conclusion, foundational time series models already provide accurate, fast, and operationally attractive zero-shot forecasts for ED occupancy. With long historical context, several models cluster in performance and surpass strong baselines, substantially lowering the barrier to adoption by removing per-site training. While multivariate zero-shot use did not help in our setting, careful covariate design and light adaptation may close that gap. Taken together, these results support zero-shot FMs as a practical starting point for ED forecasting and outline a path toward even stronger, fine-tuned, and reliably calibrated deployments.
