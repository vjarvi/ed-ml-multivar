Overcrowding in hospital emergency departments (EDs) has a clear negative impact on patient health \cite{sartini_overcrowding_2022}.
For example, a higher mortality rate \cite{berg_associations_2019,jo_emergency_2014} and an increased length of stay \cite{mccarthy_crowding_2009} have been associated with crowding. 
Accurate predictions of future ED occupancy levels could prevent crowding by enabling more effective resource allocation.

State-of-the-art (SOTA) machine learning models for ED occupancy prediction are trained using historical occupancy data and sometimes other external variables, 
like weather and traffic data \cite{tuominen_forecasting_2024,hyndman2018forecasting}.
Training machine learning models is a computationally heavy and time-consuming task. Because every hospital has unique characteristics, a 
model trained with data from one hospital may not accurately predict occupancy levels for other hospitals.
Foundational models do not need to be trained on task-specific data. If they can predict ED occupancy accurately enough, the difficult task of model training can be skipped. 
This could save a substantial amount of computation and man-hours.

This thesis evaluates foundational time series models using ED occupancy data from Acuta. It is the emergency department of Tampere University Hospital (TAYS) in Tampere, Finland. 
The data is identical to that in a study by Tuominen et al. \cite{tuominen_forecasting_2024}.
In their work, they developed machine learning models to predict ED occupancy. This allows for a direct comparison between the performance of foundational models and their results.

The goal of this thesis is to examine how well foundational time series models can forecast emergency department occupancy.
Five models are evaluated using multiple input lengths to identify the best performer.
This top model then undergoes two additional evaluations: one using multivariate data and another involving fine-tuning.

The thesis is structured as follows. Chapter 2 provides the necessary background on emergency department forecasting, time series forecasting, and foundational models.
Chapter 3 details the experimental setup, and Chapter 4 presents the results. Chapter 5 analyzes these results, and Chapter 6 concludes the results and the thesis.
