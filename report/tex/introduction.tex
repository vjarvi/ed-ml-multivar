Emergency department (ED) crowding has been widely documented to negatively impact a range of clinically relevant patient outcomes \cite{sartini_overcrowding_2022}, including higher mortality rates \cite{berg_associations_2019,jo_emergency_2014} and longer lengths of stay \cite{mccarthy_crowding_2009}. Accurate predictions of future ED occupancy levels would enable proactive resource allocation aiming to alleviate or even prevent future crowding events.

Emergency department forecasting refers to the process of predicting the future status of the ED, typically using timeseries forecasting models. Traditionally, these have been trained using historical utilization data -- sometimes with external variables such as weather and traffic data \cite{tuominen_forecasting_2024,hyndman2018forecasting}. However, training machine learning models involves a number of problems. First, every hospital has unique characteristics. A model trained with data from one hospital may not accurately predict occupancy levels for other hospitals. This means that a new model must be trained for each hospital at the time of implementation and repeatedly throughout the production cycle, which can be a computationally heavy and time-consuming task. Second, accessing the required data for each hospital can be a challenge due to either regulatory or practical reasons. 

Foundation models (FMs) are artificial intelligence (AI) systems that can be used in a wide range of different tasks and are trained on massive amounts of diverse data \cite{bommasani2022opportunitiesrisksfoundationmodels}. In the context of emergency department forecasting, they offer a set of unique advantages in comparison to traditional machine learning algorithms. First, there is no need for model training. Second, they can be deployed with minimal local data collection. Third, their massive size and training data allows them to discover patterns that traditional machine learning model would have missed. \cite{bommasani2022opportunitiesrisksfoundationmodels}

Foundational models do not need to be trained on task-specific data. If they can predict ED occupancy accurately enough, the difficult task of model training can be skipped. This could save a substantial amount of computation and man-hours.

This thesis evaluates whether the newly introduced foundational time series models can be used to predict ED utilization of Tampere University Hospital ED "Acuta." A public dataset from a GitHub repository \cite{tuominen2023edmultivar} containing the total hourly occupancy of the ED from June 20, 2018 to June 19, 2019 is used as the target variable, and the performance of the foundational models is evaluated in comparison to the state-of-the-art (SOTA) models documented by \cite{tuominen_forecasting_2024}.

The research questions of this thesis are as follows: (1) how does the performance of the FMs compare to the traditional SOTA models; 2) are there differences between the performances of the existing FM implementations, and 3) how do the FMs differ in terms of computational requirements? 

The thesis is structured as follows. Chapter 2 provides the necessary background on emergency departments, crowding, time series forecasting,and foundational models. Chapter 3 details the experimental setup and the dataset used. Chapter 4 presents the empirical results and their implications in the context of existing knowledge. Finally, Chapter 5 provides final conclusions that can be drawn from the work.
